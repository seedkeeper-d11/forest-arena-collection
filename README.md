# FOREST ARENA COLLECTION: OPEN REGISTRY COPY

*Case notes of the Registry Office. Unofficial.*

This is the registry as it stands, shuffled and untidy. The Peacekeepers have asked what the Office knows. The Office knows less than it would like and suspects more than it can prove. These are its suspicions, in the order they occurred to it.

The Office believes the thief left two things in this ledger worth recovering: a key, which opens a gate, and a code, which opens a vault. Neither is written anywhere. A key that is right looks as though someone chose it.

## WHAT THE OFFICE CAME TO SUSPECT

- **I. About silence.** For years the Office filed the registry's silences under carelessness. A careless clerk forgets at random. These silences do not look random to anyone who has stopped dismissing them.

- **II. About the numbers.** A figure in the ledger is always a figure of something. The Office's conservators have known for decades that the same number means one thing on a spear and another on a tin of medicine, and the Office's own summaries have been embarrassed by forgetting it.

- **III. About presence.** A mark on a thimble and a mark on a shield are not the same mark. The Office has never been sure how much a piece truly carries, only that it depends on more than one number.

- **IV. About the thief.** The thief did not choose at random, and not for one reason alone. Different parts of the ledger seem to describe different sides of the same decision, and now and then two quite different ways of judging a piece point at the very same piece. The Office does not believe in that sort of coincidence.

- **V. About the catalogue.** Once a piece is found, its catalogue record is a small, stubborn witness. The Office suspects it says more than it appears to, and says it only to those who already know which piece they are asking about.

- **VI. About history.** The pieces did not enter the Collection in any order the Office chose. When separate pieces of evidence have to be put together, the Office has learned to ask who arrived first.

- **VII. About what must be seen.** The first report noted the thief's taste for pieces that were well kept and of real physical presence. Nobody has ever drawn that taste. The Office suspects the thief meant it to be looked at, and that what is drawn will be a message. It also suspects the message is not whole: the thief did not trust a picture to speak alone. What it draws is nearly, but not entirely, what the vault expects. Its letters are not all of one kind, since some need no other letter to be heard. What else the vault expects, the Registry already holds.

- **VIII. About the Censor.** The Censor has been through this registry. In a few of its numbers he has left his own marks where digits should be. His habits are recorded below, torn and partly legible: each mark is a numeral he has disguised. At his gates he demands the opposite of disguise. A key is accepted only with every mark of his written as the numeral it stands for. Keys are written in capitals. Only the marks on this card are altered. Nothing else is touched.

| Mark | The Censor's habit |
|------|--------------------|
| `! S B` | Plainly recorded: they become numerals that look like them. |
| `*` | "A star whose light has gone out is a hole in the night." |
| `(` | "A fern's first curl, closing like a fist toward the ground." |
| `L` | "Tip a fallen log upside down: the ground becomes a roof." |
| `^` | "Count the poles of a lean-to." |
| `R` | "Keep the stem and the kicking leg. Open the bowl until a crossing arrow remains." |

**Keep the first key. You will need it once more at the very end.**

# CONSERVATION SCANS

*Recovered by the Conservation Office.*

These scans were stranger than they first appeared. The Office recorded every reading in pairs, twice over: once in the light a visitor sees by, and once in the light a conservator uses to see what the eye cannot. Its own saying was that the space between two things can say more than the things themselves.

The Office believes the vault holds two things: a shape and a name. The shape is a direction. The name is what you will need when you get there.

## WHAT THE OFFICE CAME TO SUSPECT

- **I. About the pairs.** The readings were recorded in related pairs, and the Office has begun to suspect that the relationships between them are more revealing than the readings themselves.

- **II. About design.** Conservation scans are meant to be dull. These are not. Something in them was put there by hand, not in any one reading, but in the way some of the readings stand to one another. Most ways of setting them side by side show nothing.

- **III. About time.** The scans were taken over ten years, by hands that changed. Taken as a single pile they are a haze. The Office suspects the years should not be allowed to blur together.

- **IV. About meaning.** When the shape is finally seen, it will be something every registrar keeps in a drawer. Do not mistake it for decoration; it is a direction. Ask what it is for, and who in the world outside the Museum still uses it to keep things where others can find them.

- **V. About the signature.** The Conservation Office never signed its work in one place. The Office suspects it signed across the years, a little at a time, and that no single year says the whole name.

- **VI. About favourites.** Every year holds hundreds of scans, and every year seems to have a favourite. It is not the first filed, nor the last, nor the most obvious. The Office never agreed how a favourite is chosen, only that the scans themselves, not the filing clerk, chose it.

- **VII. About labels.** A barcode is meant to be meaningless. The Office has come to believe its barcodes were not as meaningless as they look, and that it has never quite finished reading a single one.

- **VIII. About the name.** Whatever the years say when they finally speak, it will not look out of place on a registrar's desk.
